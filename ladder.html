How to implement

The Implementation Ladder

Organizations will be at different points in implementation, and that is expected. The goal is to be engaging in at least some strategies across all seven guidelines while working toward more comprehensive implementation — a continuum, not a pass/fail test.

Used well, the ladder highlights strengths as much as gaps: most programs find they can already see clearly where they support youth mental health today.

The five stages

What each stage means

Stage 01

Foundational

Starting point

The entry point for each guideline. A program at this stage has the basic protection or plan in place — the thing that should exist before a season starts.

Stage 02

Developing

Building momentum

The practice is no longer a one-off. Responsibility is named, training is happening, and the work is starting to repeat reliably.

Stage 03

Established

Expanding implementation

The guideline is embedded in the annual cycle — audited, surveyed, rehearsed or reviewed on a schedule rather than when someone remembers.

Stage 04

Advanced

Integrated practice

The guideline shapes how the organization runs: evaluation, hiring, referral relationships and operations all reflect it.

Stage 05

Long-term sustainability

The practice survives staff turnover and keeps improving. The program is a model others can learn from.

Two rules for using the ladder

  1. Go wide before you go deep. Begin with a foundational level of implementation across all seven guidelines, then progressively strengthen by going deeper within each one. A program at Foundational everywhere is safer than one at Advanced on two guidelines and nothing on the rest.
  2. The stages are cumulative. Each stage assumes the ones before it are in place and being maintained, not replaced.

Anticipate these

The barriers that show up every time

"We rely on volunteers"

Keep volunteer requirements accessible and time-efficient — short online modules, abbreviated preseason workshops — while holding the same standard for psychological safety. Accessibility is about format, not about lowering the bar.

Parent pressure for results

Identified in implementation research as a primary threat to developmental programming. The counter is proactive, structured education tied to your stated framework — before the season, not in response to a complaint.

No budget

Several Foundational items cost nothing but decision-making time: a written charter, a named point person, posted crisis lines, a one-page emergency plan. Mental Health First Aid runs roughly $100–200 per person — budget it as a safety line item.

It depends on one person

If the program's mental health work would collapse when a particular staff member leaves, it has not reached Developing. That stage is precisely the work of naming owners, writing it down and scheduling it.

Structural drift

Without annual evaluation against developmental standards, coaches revert to performance-focused habits. Drift is the default; the annual review is what resists it.

Coaches are already stretched

Nearly seven in ten coaches describe coaching as stressful, and most coach alongside full-time jobs. Protect their preparation time and limit administrative burden — coach well-being directly affects athlete environments.
