The Guidelines

Seven guidelines, reached by consensus. Each has its own page setting out the standard, the evidence behind it, and the implementation protocol. The ladder below shows what progress looks like on each one — from a starting point through to long-term sustainability.

The seven guidelines

Section 3

How the guidelines were developed

To develop comprehensive and practical mental health guidelines for youth sport in the U.S., the research team used a four-pronged approach that bridged academic theory and real-world application.

  1. A rigorous rapid review of the scientific literature

    Following Cochrane protocol, the team systematically analyzed thousands of academic studies focused on the U.S. context to establish a baseline of what is known about how sport affects youth mental well-being — separating anecdotal belief from evidence, and identifying which aspects of sport are protective and which pose risk.

  2. Focus groups with 60+ key actors

    Young athletes, parents/guardians, coaches and organizational leaders across the country discussed competitive pressure, social media surveillance and the need for supportive environments — surfacing the specific, practical barriers that make mental health support difficult to implement in local clubs and schools.

  3. A four-round Delphi study with 22 experts

    Sport psychologists, public health officials, coaching directors and mental health experts evaluated candidate guidelines not only on importance but on feasibility for everyday programs. Iterative voting filtered out unrealistic ideas and refined the language.

  4. A two-day in-person consensus workshop

    Sixteen panelists took part in a series of consensus votes rather than a single decision, debating exact wording and what should or should not be included between rounds. The workshop also addressed the need for ongoing evaluation and practical testing, and produced strategic activation plans for dissemination.

The panel reached consensus on seven mental health guidelines.

Glossary of key terms

  • Youth sport programsPrograms offering organized sport participation — school-based, community-based, or under a national governing body — with designated coaches, structured practices, and goal-oriented activity.
  • Mental healthA state of well-being in which the individual realizes their own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively, and is able to contribute to their community.
  • Mental health literacyKnowledge and beliefs about mental disorders which aid their recognition, management or prevention. Includes the ability to recognize specific disorders, knowing how to seek information, and reducing stigma.
  • Crisis responseImmediate, short-term assistance given to someone experiencing a mental health emergency (e.g., suicide risk, panic attack) to restore equilibrium and ensure safety until professional help is engaged.
  • SafeguardingProactive measures to ensure the environment is safe, supportive and protected from abuse.
  • Mental Health Point PersonA designated individual within an organization responsible for maintaining knowledge of mental health guidelines and acting as a liaison for resources.
  • Athlete / playerThroughout this guide, any young person participating in a sporting activity. It does not refer to any particular level of competition or talent.
