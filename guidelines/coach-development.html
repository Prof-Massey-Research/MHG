Guideline 03

Coach Development for Supportive Coaching

Youth sport programs require and support their coaches to engage in ongoing coach development in methods that promote safe and supportive coaching.

Why this matters

Coaches are among the most influential adults in young athletes' lives, shaping not only athletic development but psychological well-being, motivation, sense of belonging and self-worth. Sport's potential to foster positive youth development is realized primarily through the quality of coaching. Coaches who create psychologically healthy environments help athletes thrive; coaches who lack those skills — even with the best intentions — can inadvertently harm young people's well-being.

This guideline is deliberately proactive. Rather than training coaches primarily to identify and respond to mental health problems, it centers on equipping them to build psychologically safe, need-supportive environments that prevent distress in the first place.

What the program has to build

Research has made it clear the types of environments that can be created for young people to thrive within sport. While the coach often sets the tone on the field, the organization sets the constraints within which coaches operate. What an organization trains for, schedules, rewards and evaluates decides whether these conditions exist.

The satisfaction of three basic psychological needs

Decades of sport psychology research point to three needs that, when met, produce well-being, motivation and healthy development — and that, when undermined through controlling behavior, harsh criticism or exclusionary practice, produce anxiety, burnout and dropout.

  • Control. A sense of choice, volition and ownership over one’s actions.
  • Competence. Feeling effective, capable and able to meet challenges at an appropriate level.
  • Connection. Feeling connected to, cared for and valued by others in the environment.

Organizations should ensure that coach training covers the actual coaching behaviors and practices that ensure these needs can be satisfied in the sport environment.

A mastery oriented achievement climate

A climate is not set by individual coaches — it is set by what a program measures, rewards and celebrates. If the organization recognises records and results, a coach who wants a mastery climate is working against the structure around them.

Eliminating comparison entirely is rarely possible in sport. But athletes thrive where there is a clear focus on personal growth, learning and improvement. These features of a mastery environment are consistently associated with higher enjoyment, sustained motivation, lower anxiety and continued participation. Coach training should emphasize the behaviors that are aligned with a mastery environment, and how these differ from an environment focused on social comparisons and winning no matter what the cost. Coaches should leave training understanding what they emphasize, reward, and punish will have large implications for the psychosocial development of athletes.

Implementation protocol

  1. Require comprehensive coach development training

    Coaches spend the most direct time with athletes and shape the daily environment. Training must address not only what coaches know but how they behave — integrating evidence-based practices for supporting autonomy, building competence, fostering connection, and creating psychological safety for all athletes.

    Implementation list
    • All coaches complete evidence-based coach development training before beginning their roles or within their first season.
      Recommended training partners:
      • Center for Healing & Justice Through Sport — trauma-informed practice, racial equity and healing-centered environments, on a neuroscience-based model.
      • Coach Beyond — The Ohio State University. Mental health literacy and supportive coaching, built for sport staff.
      • Positive Coaching Alliance — self-determination-theory-grounded training, Double-Goal Coach workshops and mastery climate resources.
    • Mandate refresher training or continuing education every 2–3 years.
    • Prioritize training formats that go beyond information delivery to include skill practice, self-reflection and application planning.

  2. Build reflection and feedback into the season

    One-time training does not change behavior. Coaches need structured chances to see what they are actually doing — their own read of it, and someone else’s — and a conversation that follows from both.

    Implementation list
    • Equip coaches with dedicated time and tools to evaluate their own coaching behaviors. Coach Self-Reflection Journal →
    • Facilitate coach observations and peer feedback focused on psychological climate. Coaching Climate Observation Form →
    • Compare the two: where a coach’s own assessment and an observer’s record differ is usually where the useful conversation is.
    • Observe the same coach more than once a season, so you are looking at a pattern rather than one session.
    Coach Self-Reflection Journal Coaching Climate Observation Form

  3. Create structures for peer learning

    Coaches learn most from other coaches working with the same athletes under the same constraints. Left to chance this does not happen; it has to be scheduled and staffed like anything else.

    Implementation list
    • Organize monthly or quarterly coach learning communities for reflection, challenge-sharing and problem-solving.
    • Create mentor–mentee relationships between experienced need-supportive coaches and newer coaches.
    • Host an annual learning day featuring researchers, experienced coaches and athlete voices.
    • Provide a coach resource library with research summaries, strategy guides and video examples. Nike Coaching →
    • Offer all of the above in formats that fit the people you actually have — online, evening and weekend options for coaches holding other jobs.

  4. Protect coach well-being

    A depleted coach cannot create a need-supportive environment, whatever their training. Most youth sport coaching is done by volunteers around other work, and a program that ignores this gets the coaching that follows from it.

    Implementation list
    • Provide resources for stress management and burnout prevention.
    • Protect coaching as a practice by limiting administrative burden and providing adequate preparation time.
    • Be explicit about what is expected of a volunteer coach and what is not, while holding the same standard on psychological safety for everyone.

  5. Embed principles in program structures and evaluation

    Sustainable culture change requires that these principles live in organizational systems, not individual initiative. When evaluation, recognition and accountability align with the values, the organization signals that psychological well-being is a core priority.

    Implementation list
    • Create evaluation processes that assess coaches on their ability to create positive, inclusive environments — not only win–loss records.
    • Gather anonymous athlete feedback on their experiences of autonomy, competence, relatedness and psychological safety.
    • Recognize and celebrate coaches who exemplify need-supportive, inclusive coaching through awards, acknowledgment or leadership opportunities.
    • Address coaching behaviors that undermine psychological well-being through clear accountability processes.
