Guideline 03

Coach Development for Safe Environments

Youth sport programs require and support their coaches to engage in ongoing coach development in methods that promote safe and supportive coaching.

Why this matters

Coaches are among the most influential adults in young athletes' lives, shaping not only athletic development but psychological well-being, motivation, sense of belonging and self-worth. Sport's potential to foster positive youth development is realized primarily through the quality of coaching. Coaches who create psychologically healthy environments help athletes thrive; coaches who lack those skills — even with the best intentions — can inadvertently harm young people's well-being.

This guideline is deliberately proactive. Rather than training coaches primarily to identify and respond to mental health problems, it centers on equipping them to build psychologically safe, need-supportive environments that prevent distress in the first place.

Key concepts

The three basic psychological needs

Decades of sport psychology research point to three fundamental needs that, when satisfied, produce well-being, motivation and healthy development. When coaches undermine them — through controlling behaviors, harsh criticism or exclusionary practices — athletes experience diminished well-being, anxiety, burnout and dropout.

Control

Feeling a sense of choice, volition and ownership over one's actions.

Competence

Feeling effective, capable and able to meet challenges at an appropriate level.

Connection

Feeling connected to, cared for and valued by others in the environment.

Mastery climate vs. performance climate

Mastery climate

  • Personal improvement and effort
  • Learning from mistakes
  • Collaboration and growth
  • Effort-based evaluation
  • Errors as learning opportunities

Performance climate

  • Social comparison and rankings
  • Mistakes are punished
  • Only winning matters
  • Outperforming teammates
  • Fear-based motivation

Eliminating comparison entirely is rarely possible in sport. But athletes thrive when they experience a clear focus on personal growth, learning and improvement. Strong mastery climates are consistently associated with higher enjoyment, sustained motivation, lower anxiety and continued participation.

Psychological safety

Athletes' well-being is protected when they can be themselves, take interpersonal risks, and trust that they will not be punished, humiliated or marginalized. This is especially critical for athletes who face systemic barriers to participation — LGBTQ+ athletes, immigrants, athletes with racialized identities, those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds and athletes with disabilities — who often carry additional stressors including discrimination, stereotyping, pressure to hide their identity, microaggressions, lack of representation, and cultures that tolerate harmful language. Creating psychological safety requires intentional, ongoing effort.

Implementation protocol

  1. Require comprehensive coach development training

    Coaches spend the most direct time with athletes and shape the daily environment. Training must address not only what coaches know but how they behave — integrating evidence-based practices for supporting autonomy, building competence, fostering connection, and creating psychological safety for all athletes.

    Implementation list
    • All coaches complete evidence-based coach development training before beginning their roles or within their first season.
    • Mandate refresher training or continuing education every 2–3 years.
    • Make psychological well-being and athlete development non-negotiable components of coach certification, equal in importance to technical and tactical knowledge.
    • Prioritize training formats that go beyond information delivery to include skill practice, self-reflection and application planning.

    Concrete coaching behaviors training should build

    Supporting control (autonomy) — offer meaningful choices within structure; give rationales for rules rather than demanding compliance; acknowledge athletes' perspectives even when holding boundaries; use informational and invitational language over controlling language; encourage athlete-initiated goal setting and self-evaluation.

    Building competence — set developmentally appropriate challenges that are difficult but achievable; give specific, informational feedback rather than vague praise or criticism; recognize effort and improvement, not just outcomes; break skills into manageable steps; frame errors as learning opportunities.

    Creating mastery climates — define success as personal improvement and maximum effort; value every team member's contribution rather than only praising stars; encourage athletes to support each other's development; help athletes set process goals, not only outcome goals.

    Fostering connection (relatedness) — create regular opportunities to check in and ask about life outside sport; build cohesion through cooperative activities and shared experiences; model respectful relationships and address relational harm; be emotionally available and responsive.

    Cultural competence and inclusive practice — training on coaching LGBTQ+ athletes, affirming language, and addressing homophobia and transphobia; education on coaching across racial and ethnic differences; recognizing and addressing microaggressions and intervening when witnessing discrimination; developing awareness of coaches' own social identities; structured opportunities to learn from athletes' lived experience through anonymous feedback, listening sessions or advisory groups.

    Differentiate by context

    • School programs — integrate into professional development days; align with state requirements where possible; leverage relationships with sport psychology faculty.
    • Club programs — work with national governing bodies to embed content into existing certification pathways; require training before hiring.
    • Recreational programs — offer accessible online modules (1–2 hours) with concrete strategies; provide abbreviated in-person workshops before the season.

  2. Provide ongoing learning, peer support and coach well-being resources

    One-time training is insufficient to sustain behavior change. Coaches need ongoing opportunities to reflect, learn from peers and receive support — and their own well-being directly affects their capacity to create positive environments.

    Implementation list
    • Equip coaches with dedicated time and tools to evaluate their own coaching behaviors.
    • Organize monthly or quarterly coach learning communities for reflection, challenge-sharing and problem-solving.
    • Create mentor–mentee relationships between experienced need-supportive coaches and newer coaches.
    • Facilitate coach observations and peer feedback focused on psychological climate.
    • Provide a coach resource library with research summaries, strategy guides and video examples.
    • Host an annual learning day featuring researchers, experienced coaches and athlete voices.
    • Offer flexible formats (online, evening, weekend) to fit coaches' schedules.
    • Provide resources for stress management and burnout prevention.
    • Set realistic expectations for volunteer coaches while maintaining high standards for psychological safety.
    • Protect coaching as a practice by limiting administrative burden and providing adequate preparation time.

  3. Build team rituals and shared experiences

    Rituals and shared experiences strengthen relatedness and create collective identity. They move need-supportive coaching from individual behaviors into team culture, giving athletes belonging, meaning and ownership that outlast any single interaction.

    Implementation list
    • Establish regular rituals that build connection and meaning — pre-game routines, post-practice check-ins, celebration traditions, season-opening and closing ceremonies.
    • Create shared experiences beyond competition: team meals, community service, social activities, team-building challenges.
    • Involve athletes in creating and maintaining traditions, giving them ownership and voice.
    • Use rituals to mark transitions and milestones — welcoming new members, honoring departing athletes, recognizing growth.
    • Ensure rituals are inclusive and do not inadvertently exclude athletes on the basis of identity, ability or socioeconomic status.

  4. Embed principles in program structures and evaluation

    Sustainable culture change requires that these principles live in organizational systems, not individual initiative. When evaluation, recognition and accountability align with the values, the organization signals that psychological well-being is a core priority.

    Implementation list
    • Create evaluation processes that assess coaches on their ability to create positive, inclusive environments — not only win–loss records.
    • Gather anonymous athlete feedback on their experiences of autonomy, competence, relatedness and psychological safety.
    • Recognize and celebrate coaches who exemplify need-supportive, inclusive coaching through awards, acknowledgment or leadership opportunities.
    • Address coaching behaviors that undermine psychological well-being through clear accountability processes.

Recommended training programs

  • Center for Healing & Justice Through Sport — chjs.orgTrauma-informed practices, racial equity, healing-centered environments. Neuroscience-based model.
  • NFHS Learning Center — nfhslearn.comOnline courses on positive behavior and respectful environments. Required by many state associations.
  • Nike Coaching — nike.com/coachingFree coaching philosophy, athlete development, inclusive coaching toolkit and session plans.
  • Positive Coaching Alliance — positivecoach.orgSelf-determination-theory-grounded training. Double-Goal Coach workshops, mastery climate resources.
  • USOPC Coaching Education — teamusa.org/coaching-educationMulti-level NGB certification with an athlete-centered philosophy.
  • Athlete Ally — athleteally.orgLGBTQ+ inclusive sport training. Pronoun guide, transgender athlete resources, policy templates.

Additional resources

  • Women's Sports Foundation — womenssportsfoundation.orgResearch and guidance on coaching girls and women. Free.
  • Association for Applied Sport Psychology — appliedsportpsych.orgMotivational climate resources and certified consultant directory. Some free; consultation fees vary.
  • Self-Determination Theory Resources — selfdeterminationtheory.orgOfficial SDT research repository including sport-specific materials. Free.
