Guideline 01

Safeguarding & Abuse Prevention

Youth sport programs comply with best practices in safeguarding and abuse prevention.

Why this matters

The welfare of youth in sport must extend beyond physical injury prevention to encompass a comprehensive safeguarding framework. This standard begins with rigorous screening (e.g., background checks, references) for all staff and volunteers. Organizations must equip all personnel with training to prevent, identify, respond to, and report emotional, physical, and sexual abuse. A robust code of conduct must underpin this training.

However, true safeguarding extends beyond compliance with minimum requirements. It requires building a culture where everyone — coaches, administrators, parents/guardians, and athletes — understand that protecting young people is a collective responsibility. This means moving from a checklist mentality to an organizational culture that prioritizes continuous improvement, empowers bystanders to intervene, establishes clear behavioral standards, and removes barriers to reporting concerns.

Guideline implementation protocol

  1. Complete background checks

    Background checks are the foundational screening tool to prevent individuals with histories of abuse or misconduct from accessing youth. This is the minimum compliance requirement and establishes a baseline of safety before individuals begin working with young people.

    Implementation list
    • Conduct comprehensive background checks on all staff and volunteers who work directly with young people before they begin their roles.
    • Background checks should include: criminal history searches (state and national), sex offender registry checks, and employment reference verification.
    • Establish clear disqualification criteria based on the nature and severity of offenses.
    • Re-screen all staff and volunteers at regular intervals, typically every 2–3 years.
    • Maintain secure, confidential records of all background checks in compliance with applicable laws.
    • Consult the National Council of Youth Sports (NCYS) Guidelines for recommended best practices on screening and selection.

  2. Require SafeSport or equivalent abuse prevention training

    Training equips staff with the knowledge to recognize warning signs of abuse, understand grooming behaviors, respond appropriately to disclosures, and fulfill their legal reporting obligations. This moves beyond screening to active prevention and awareness, creating a first line of defense against abuse.

    Implementation list
    • All staff and volunteers who work directly with young people must complete evidence-based abuse prevention training (such as U.S. Center for SafeSport training) before beginning their roles.
    • Training must cover: recognizing signs and symptoms of abuse, understanding boundary violations and grooming behaviors, responding to disclosures from youth, mandated reporting requirements and procedures, and organizational safeguarding policies.
    • Require refresher training every 1–2 years to reinforce concepts and update staff on policy changes.
    • Track completion of training and maintain records as part of staff credentialing.

  3. Conduct annual safeguarding audits using a self-assessment tool

    Moving from compliance to culture requires regular evaluation and continuous improvement. An annual audit helps organizations identify gaps in their safeguarding practices, measure progress over time, and demonstrate commitment to ongoing development. This strategy shifts the focus from 'checking boxes' to building robust systems that evolve with emerging best practices.

    Implementation list
    • Designate a safeguarding lead or committee responsible for conducting the annual audit.
    • Use a comprehensive safeguarding audit checklist to evaluate current practices across key domains: staff screening and selection, training and education, supervision practices, communication and social media policies, reporting procedures and documentation, physical environment and facility safety, and code of conduct implementation.
    • Complete the audit annually, documenting both strengths and areas for improvement.
    • Consider sharing audit results (in appropriate summary form) with stakeholders including parents/guardians to demonstrate transparency and accountability.

  4. Establish clear codes of conduct with specific behavioral standards

    Vague policies leave too much to interpretation and make it difficult to address boundary violations early. A detailed code of conduct with specific, observable behavioral standards removes ambiguity, empowers staff to recognize violations, and provides clear grounds for accountability. This creates a shared understanding of acceptable behavior and reduces the 'gray zones' where abuse can occur and thrive.

    Implementation list

    Develop a comprehensive code of conduct that addresses specific high-risk situations with concrete behavioral standards. These situations may include:

    • One-on-one interactions
    • Electronic communications and social media
    • Travel and overnight stays
    • Physical contact and spotting
    • Locker room and changing area supervision
    • Photography and video

    Require all staff and volunteers to sign the code of conduct annually as a condition of participation.

    Review the code during staff onboarding and reference specific standards during regular team meetings and training sessions.

    Share relevant portions of the code of conduct with parents/guardians and athletes so everyone understands expectations.

    Establish clear consequences for code violations, applied consistently regardless of an individual's status or tenure.

    Code of Conduct

  5. Centralize communication on a single documented platform

    Grooming depends on private, unobserved contact, and a phone is the easiest unobserved space in a program. Routing all coach–athlete and coach–family communication through one platform where messages are logged and visible to administrators applies the same logic as the Rule of Three to digital contact: it makes the transparency standard enforceable rather than aspirational, and it means concerning patterns can be seen early rather than reconstructed after a disclosure.

    Implementation list
    • Select one platform for all team business — scheduling, logistics, performance feedback and family updates — and require its use rather than personal phones, personal email or personal social accounts.
    • Give administrators standing visibility of communication on that platform, not access granted only once a concern has been raised.
    • Copy a parent or guardian on communication with athletes under 18, or require that it take place in a channel administrators can already see.
    • Set the hours during which communication normally takes place, with exceptions only for emergencies.
    • Prohibit private direct messages between staff and individual athletes on personal accounts.
    • Retain records for the period set by your retention policy, so that patterns can be reviewed if a concern is raised later.
    • Write the platform rules into your code of conduct, and check them in the annual safeguarding audit alongside the other communication standards.
    Code of Conduct

  6. Implement bystander intervention protocols

    Culture change happens when everyone in the organization feels empowered and equipped to speak up when they witness concerning behavior. Bystander intervention moves safeguarding from an individual responsibility to a collective one. Many boundary violations occur in the 'gray zone' where behavior is inappropriate but not clearly abusive: these moments require intervention before escalation. Research shows that most abuse could be prevented if bystanders acted when they first witnessed warning signs.

    Implementation list
    • Train all staff, volunteers, and when age-appropriate, athletes themselves in the principles of effective bystander intervention: recognizing concerning situations, overcoming common barriers to action (diffusion of responsibility, fear of misinterpreting, social consequences), and choosing safe and effective intervention strategies.
    • Develop and distribute pocket-sized reference guides with specific scripts for common scenarios, such as: inappropriate communication or social media contact, boundary violations during travel or overnight stays, one-on-one situations without proper protocols, verbal or emotional abuse of an athlete, and concerns about a colleague's behavior toward youth.
    • Create a culture where intervention is expected and supported by leadership. Publicly recognize and thank individuals who speak up about concerns.
    • Provide multiple channels for reporting concerns, including anonymous options, to reduce barriers to intervention.

    Practice bystander intervention scenarios during staff training and team meetings to build confidence and familiarity with intervention techniques.

    Protect staff from retaliation when they report concerns in good faith, even if investigations do not substantiate allegations.

Key evidence and supporting research

Researchers have documented that youth sport environments carry potential for harm, particularly when system-level factors go unaddressed. Additional research has identified harassment and abuse in sport as a public health concern, with coach–athlete power imbalances and competitive pressures elevating risk for youth athletes. Policy frameworks from organizations including the U.S. Center for SafeSport, Keeping Children Safe, and the IOC converge on a core set of baseline protections that include pre-engagement screening, mandatory abuse prevention training, accessible reporting mechanisms, and codes of conduct. Evidence from youth-serving sectors more broadly show that background screening reduces access by individuals with prior abuse histories, and that training improves staff knowledge and reporting confidence.
