State High School Athletic Rules

Dead periods, out-of-season contact limits and practice requirements are set by your state association, not by your program. The specifics vary by state, but the shape does not — there are four questions to answer, and this is how to answer them for your program and keep the answers current.

Supports Guideline 02 — Long-Term Athlete Development

Read the guideline

How these rules work

Your state association, not your program, defines the frame a season sits in: when it may begin, when competition may start, what a coach may do with athletes outside it, and when contact must stop altogether. Your own scheduling policy sits inside that frame. Anything you set is in addition to the association’s rules, never instead of them.

The details differ by state and are revised most years, but the shape is consistent enough that you can prepare for the conversation before you open your handbook. There are four questions to answer, and the answers to all four live in two documents: your association’s handbook or bylaws, and its sports calendar.

The four questions to answer

QuestionLook for it underWhy it matters developmentally
When must we stop entirely? Dead period · moratorium · no-contact period · blackout Protected time away from the sport. Treat it as recovery the season is built around, not an interruption to work around.
What may we do out of season? Out-of-season contact · open gym rules · contact days · non-traditional season Where year-round creep starts. Limits here are what keep multi-sport participation possible.
What must happen before the first contest? Required practices · acclimatisation · heat policy A required lead-in exists because bodies need staged exposure. It is a floor, not a target.
How long, and how much? Season start and end dates · contest maximums · participation limits The ceiling on total load. Your own maximums should sit at or below it, by stage.

The short version

Find your association’s handbook and sports calendar once. Answer the four questions above, write the answers on the worksheet below, and date them. Re-check before each season, because associations revise these annually. If your own policy is stricter than the association’s on any line, say so explicitly so nobody reads the looser rule and assumes it applies.

Explaining this to families

These limits read as bureaucracy unless the reason is given. When you publish your season plan, say why the constraints exist: they protect recovery, they stop the season expanding to fill the year, and they are the floor your program builds on rather than a ceiling on commitment. A program that explains a dead period as protected rest is making the same argument as its developmental charter.

Find your state association

Rules and dates are set by the association that governs your schools, and are revised most years, so the current handbook is always the authority. This page does not reproduce any state’s rules for that reason — values copied from 51 handbooks would go stale without any signal to you about which ones had.
