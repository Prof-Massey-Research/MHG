Appendix 5.3 · Slide deck outline

Parent/Guardian Orientation Outline

A seven-slide preseason orientation that shifts parents and guardians from performance critics to developmental partners.

Supports Guideline 02 — Long-Term Athlete Development

Read the guideline

Why this session matters

A mandatory parent/guardian orientation is a critical step in protecting the long-term goals of a program from the pressure of short-term, win-at-all-costs thinking. Parent/guardian pressure for immediate competitive results is repeatedly identified in implementation research as a major threat to developmental programming.

By providing structured education tied to your program's framework, you shift parents and guardians from being performance critics to supportive partners. A mandatory pre-season session ensures everyone understands the why behind your methods — the science of youth development, the risks of early specialization, and the benefits of playing multiple sports.

Practical notes

  • Run it before the first practice, not after the first complaint.
  • Keep it to 35–45 minutes with time for questions.
  • Offer a recorded version for families who cannot attend live.
  • Provide materials in the primary languages spoken by your families.

Slide 1 · Welcome and our mission

  • Brief introduction of the program leadership.
  • Our core goal: building healthy, resilient athletes who love the sport.

Slide 2 · The problem with hustle culture in youth sport

  • The stat: children who specialize early are significantly more likely to burn out, suffer overuse injuries, and drop out of sport entirely by age 13.
  • The message: we are actively working against the "more is always better" mentality.

Slide 3 · What is LTAD / ADM?

  • Define it simply: a science-backed blueprint that matches the demands of the sport to the biological and psychological readiness of the child.
  • Analogy: you don't teach calculus to a second-grader. You shouldn't run elite-level travel schedules for a nine-year-old.

Slide 4 · What this looks like for your child this season

  • Show the actual schedule: exact hours per week, practice-to-game ratio, mandatory rest days.
  • Explain why the ratio favors practice. For example: "In a 60-minute game, a player might touch the ball for 90 seconds. In a 60-minute practice, they touch it for 20 minutes. Practice builds skill."

Slide 5 · Redefining success

  • Give stage-specific goals. For a ten-year-old, success is mastering a fundamental movement, being a good teammate, and wanting to come back next year.
  • Winning is a by-product of good development, not the primary goal.

Slide 6 · How you can help

  • The car-ride-home rule: avoid critiquing the game. Ask "Did you have fun?" and "What did you learn today?"
  • Monitor the training load: tell us if your child is excessively fatigued, sore, or losing enthusiasm.

Slide 7 · Q&A and next steps

  • Open floor for questions.
  • Collect signed Developmental Commitment Pledges.
  • Share the Mental Health Point Person's contact details and the resource directory.
