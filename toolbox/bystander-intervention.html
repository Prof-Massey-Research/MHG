Appendix 5.1 · Training guide

Bystander Intervention Protocols

The 5 D's framework, the barriers that stop people acting, and six practiced scenarios with specific language to use.

Supports Guideline 01 — Safeguarding & Abuse Prevention

Read the guideline

Why bystander intervention matters

Research shows that most abuse could be prevented if bystanders acted when they first witnessed warning signs. Safeguarding is everyone's responsibility, not just that of administrators or designated leaders. When staff, volunteers, parents/guardians and athletes feel empowered to speak up, inappropriate behavior gets interrupted before it escalates.

Many boundary violations occur in the gray zone — behaviors that are inappropriate but not clearly abusive. These are precisely the moments that require intervention.

The 5 D's of bystander intervention

When you witness concerning behavior you have five strategies. Choose the one that is safest and most effective for the situation.

1. Direct — address it head-on

Speak directly to the person engaging in concerning behavior. Works best when you have positional authority or a good relationship with the individual.

When to use: minor boundary violations, coaching moments, situations where direct address feels safe.

"Coach, I noticed you're texting Sarah late at night. Our policy requires parents/guardians to be copied on all communications with athletes."

"Hey, I see you're working one-on-one with Jake behind closed doors. Let's move to the gym where others can see."

"That comment about Emma's body isn't appropriate. Let's focus feedback on her athletic performance."

2. Distract — interrupt the situation

Create an interruption that stops the behavior without directly confronting it. This gives you time to assess and lets the person redirect.

When to use: when direct confrontation feels unsafe, when you are unsure whether intervention is needed, or when you want to de-escalate without conflict.

Walking into a room where an adult and athlete are alone: "Oh, Coach Martinez, the athletic director is looking for you!"

Interrupting uncomfortable physical contact: "Hey coach, can I grab you for a quick question about tomorrow's schedule?"

Redirecting during an inappropriate conversation: "Sorry to interrupt, but we need to start the next drill!"

3. Delegate — get help from others

Report to someone with more authority or expertise. You do not have to handle everything yourself.

When to use: serious concerns, situations beyond your authority, when you need backup, or when an initial intervention has not worked.

To the safeguarding lead: "I've noticed Coach Davis has been giving individual athletes rides home alone. Can you address this?"

To the athletic director: "I'm concerned about how Coach Lee speaks to athletes. I've heard multiple instances of yelling and name-calling."

To a supervisor: "An athlete disclosed something concerning to me. I need guidance on next steps."

4. Delay — check in after the fact

If you cannot intervene in the moment, follow up afterward. Check on the athlete's well-being and gather information to report or address the concern later.

When to use: missed opportunities, assessing athlete well-being, gathering information before formal reporting.

To the athlete: "Hey Jordan, I noticed Coach seemed really hard on you today. Are you okay? Is there anything you want to talk about?"

To a colleague: "I saw you and Alex alone in the equipment room earlier. Just a reminder that our policy requires another adult present."

5. Document — record what you observed

Write down what you saw, heard or experienced. Documentation creates a record for reporting, identifies patterns over time, and supports investigations. Do this always, in addition to whichever other method you use.

Record: date, time and location · individuals involved and their roles · specific behaviors observed (what was said or done) · context · any witnesses · actions you took · who you reported to and when.

Overcoming common barriers

BarrierHow to overcome it
"I might be misinterpreting the situation"Trust your instincts. If something feels wrong, it probably is. You don't need certainty — just reasonable concern. Report it and let investigators determine the facts.
"It's not my place to say something"Safeguarding is everyone's responsibility. Your organization expects and supports intervention. You are not overstepping; you are fulfilling a duty to protect youth.
"I don't want to damage someone's reputation"Protecting children takes priority over protecting adult reputations. Confidential reporting processes exist to investigate fairly while protecting privacy.
"Someone else will handle it"Don't assume. Bystander research shows people are less likely to intervene when others are present. You might be the only one who acts.
"I'm afraid of retaliation"Your organization prohibits retaliation against good-faith reporting. Use anonymous reporting if needed, or choose a method that feels safer, such as distract or delegate.
"The person is well-respected or has been here a long time"Abusers often cultivate positive reputations to deflect suspicion. Position and tenure don't exempt anyone. Behavior matters more than status.

Practice scenarios

Review these regularly and practice them with colleagues during training.

Scenario 1

Inappropriate electronic communication

Situation: you notice a coach texting an individual athlete at 10:30 PM. The athlete's parent/guardian is not copied on the messages.

Direct — "Coach, I noticed you're texting Sarah late in the evening. Our communication policy requires messages to athletes under 18 to copy parents/guardians and occur between 7 AM and 9 PM. Can we review the policy together?"

Delegate — "I've become aware that Coach Martinez is texting athletes late at night without copying parents/guardians. This violates our communication policy. Can you address this?"

Document — "On [date] at approximately 10:30 PM, I observed Coach Martinez texting athlete [name] (age 15) on a personal phone. Parent/guardian was not copied."

Scenario 2

One-on-one behind a closed door

Situation: you walk past an office and notice a coach meeting alone with an athlete behind a closed door with no window visibility.

Distract — Knock and enter: "Sorry to interrupt — Coach, do you have a second? I need a hand with the equipment before the next group arrives."

Direct — "Coach, our policy requires one-on-one meetings to be observable. Let's move to a room with windows, or keep the door open."

Delay — Check in with the athlete afterward: "I saw you were meeting with Coach earlier. Everything okay? You can always come to me if something feels off."

Scenario 3

Inappropriate physical contact

Situation: you observe a coach giving an athlete a prolonged, full-frontal hug and rubbing their back. The athlete looks uncomfortable.

Distract — "Hey Alex, can you help me with the equipment?" or "Coach Thompson, they need you at the front desk."

Direct — Privately: "Coach, I noticed the way you were hugging Alex. Our policy limits physical contact to side hugs, high-fives and fist bumps. Let's stick to those boundaries."

Delegate — "I observed Coach Thompson engaging in inappropriate physical contact with an athlete — a prolonged full-frontal hug with back rubbing. The athlete appeared uncomfortable. This needs to be addressed immediately."

Delay — "Alex, I noticed Coach gave you a big hug earlier and you seemed uncomfortable. Are you okay? You can always say no to physical contact."

Scenario 4

Verbal abuse or humiliation

Situation: during practice you hear a coach yelling at an athlete, calling them "worthless" and "pathetic" in front of the entire team.

Distract — "Coach Davis, can I speak with you for a moment?" [pull them aside] or "Team, let's take a five-minute water break."

Direct — Privately after practice: "Coach, I need to talk about what happened today. Calling Taylor 'worthless' and 'pathetic' crosses the line into verbal abuse. We can't speak to athletes that way. This violates our code of conduct."

Delegate — "I witnessed Coach Davis verbally abusing an athlete during practice — calling them 'worthless' and 'pathetic' in front of the team. This is emotional abuse and needs immediate intervention."

Delay — "Taylor, I'm sorry you were spoken to that way. That was not okay. How are you feeling? Would you like to talk to someone about it?"

Scenario 5

Transportation alone with an athlete

Situation: you see a coach offering a single athlete a ride home after practice, with no other adults or athletes in the vehicle.

Distract — "I'm heading that direction anyway — I can give Chris a ride so you don't have to make the extra trip, Coach." Or: "Let me call Chris's parent/guardian to let them know about the change in pickup."

Direct — "Coach, our transportation policy says athletes shouldn't ride alone with a single adult. Either another athlete or adult comes along, or we contact the parent/guardian first."

Document — "On [date], Coach Wilson gave an athlete a ride home from practice alone. No other adults or athletes were present. Parent/guardian was not notified in advance."

Scenario 6

Gift-giving to an individual athlete

Situation: you notice a coach giving expensive personal gifts — clothes, electronics — to one particular athlete, outside of team recognition.

Direct — "Coach, I noticed you gave Jamie an expensive gift. Our policy prohibits individual gifts to avoid creating special relationships or the appearance of favoritism. Team gifts distributed to everyone are fine; individual ones aren't."

Delegate — "I've observed Coach Anderson giving expensive personal gifts to one athlete. This appears to be creating a special relationship and may be grooming behavior. This needs to be investigated."

Delay — "Jamie, I noticed Coach has been giving you gifts. How do you feel about that? Does it make you uncomfortable at all?" Document the frequency and value.
