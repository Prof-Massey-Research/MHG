Planning worksheet

Mapping Your Referral Network

Before you collect phone numbers, work out who you actually need on the other end of them. This worksheet takes a program from the young people it serves to the specific kinds of help it has to be able to reach.

Supports Guideline 06 — Locating Resources & Services

Read the guideline

What this worksheet is for

Most referral lists are built from whoever is easiest to find — the three counseling practices at the top of a search result, the number a board member happened to have. That list is not wrong so much as untested. It answers the question who is nearby when the question a family asks is who can see my child, this month, in our language, for what we can pay.

This worksheet is the thinking step in front of the list. Work through it once, with two or three people who know the program's families, and you will finish with a short specification: the kinds of provider this program has to be able to reach, which of them you already have, and which are missing. Filling the list in comes after.

Write about the population, never about a person

Every row below describes groups, not individuals. Do not record an athlete's name, diagnosis, immigration status, sexual orientation, gender identity, or family circumstances anywhere in this document. If you can work out which young person a line refers to, rewrite the line. This worksheet is likely to be emailed, printed and left on a desk; it must stay safe to leave there.

Part 1 · Who the program actually serves

Fill this in from what you can evidence, not from impression. Registration forms in aggregate, a parent survey, district or school demographic reports, and public census data for your catchment are all reasonable sources. Where you genuinely do not know, write “unknown” — an honest gap is more useful than a confident guess, and it tells you what to ask next season.

Notes on three rows that are easy to get wrong

Languages. The language a parent prefers for a conversation about their child's mental health is often not the language they use to arrange a ride to practice. Ask which language a family would want for a difficult conversation, not which one they get by in.

LGBTQ+ young people. You are not counting individuals and you should not try. Assume they are in your program, because they are, and specify provision accordingly.

Ability to pay. “Insured” is not the same as “able to afford care”. A plan with a high deductible, a narrow behavioral health network, or no in-network child psychiatrist can leave a family paying in full. Record what coverage your families actually hold if you can find out, and assume some cannot pay anything.

Part 3 · Who the counselor is

Families frequently ask for someone who will understand them without having to be taught — a clinician of the same race, who shares a faith, who is queer themselves, who grew up speaking the same language, or who has played sport at the level their child plays. That request is reasonable and worth taking seriously when you build the list, because the practical question is not whether therapy works in the abstract but whether a particular young person will go back for a second appointment.

Handle it carefully. A clinician's own identity is theirs to disclose, not something you collect and file.

What to ask a practice

  • “Can a family request a clinician who shares their cultural or linguistic background?”
  • “What does your team look like, and what backgrounds do your clinicians bring?”
  • “What training have your clinicians had in culturally responsive care?”
  • “If a young person or family asks to switch clinician, what happens?”
  • “Do you have clinicians who work regularly with LGBTQ+ young people?”

What not to do

  • Do not assign a provider on the basis of an athlete's identity without asking the family what they want. Some families specifically prefer a clinician from outside their own community, usually for privacy.
  • Do not disclose anything about a young person's identity in order to arrange a match. Let the family say what they want to say.
  • Do not treat one clinician as a representative of an entire community, or as the only person your program will refer certain athletes to.
  • Do not promise a family a particular clinician before confirming that person has capacity.

Part 5 · Gaps and who closes them

Every program finishes Part 2 with capabilities marked essential that nobody on the list provides. Name them here rather than hoping they resolve. A gap with an owner and a date is a plan; a gap without one is a surprise waiting for the worst week of the season.

Where to look when a gap will not close locally

  • Your school district's mental health liaison, who often knows providers a web search will not surface.
  • The community mental health center for your county — usually the backstop for uninsured families.
  • Federally Qualified Health Centers, which are required to offer sliding-scale fees.
  • University training clinics, where supervised graduate clinicians offer low-cost care.
  • Telehealth services, which can solve language and specialism gaps that local geography cannot.
  • SAMHSA's National Helpline on 1-800-662-4357, which makes local treatment referrals in multiple languages.

Part 6 · Keeping it alive

A referral list decays quietly. Clinicians move, practices stop taking a plan, a wait of two weeks becomes four months, and nobody finds out until a family needs it. Give the list an owner and a review date, and re-run a short version of Part 4 each season.

Review off-cycle, without waiting for the date, whenever any of these happen: a referral fails or a family reports they could not get seen; a provider tells you their capacity or insurance participation has changed; your program starts serving a new community, site or age group; or a crisis exposes something the list did not cover.

Where this goes next

This worksheet produces a specification, not a directory. Once it is done, the answers belong in the places people will actually reach for:

  • Our Local Mental Health Contacts, in the Partner Portal — the working list itself, with names, numbers, what each provider handles, and notes on insurance, ages and wait times.
  • Section 5 of your Mental Health Emergency Action Plan — the community resources that have to be reachable in a crisis.
  • The Website “Get Help” Widget and Bathroom Stall Poster — the public-facing version, for athletes and families who will never ask a coach.

What this worksheet is not

  • It is not a directory. It tells you what to go and find; it does not find it.
  • It does not qualify anyone to assess or diagnose. Deciding what a young person needs is the clinician's job, and the boundary is covered in Triage vs. Therapy.
  • It is not a guarantee of care. A provider on your list may still have no capacity on the day you call, which is why the list needs depth in every category that matters.

Using this worksheet

Work through it with two or three people who know the program's families — a coach, whoever handles registration, and the Mental Health Point Person if you have one. It usually takes about ninety minutes. Use Download PDF or Download as Word above to produce a copy you can fill in and circulate. Review anything you adopt against your organization's privacy obligations before you store it.
