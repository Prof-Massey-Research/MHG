Appendix 5.2 · Reflection tool

Coach Self-Reflection Journal

A structured post-session review grounded in Coach Effectiveness Training research by Ronald Smith and Frank Smoll. For the coach's own growth — not external evaluation.

Supports Guideline 03 — Coach Development

Read the guideline

Purpose and background

This journal is grounded in over four decades of research by sport psychologists Ronald Smith and Frank Smoll at the University of Washington. Their Coach Effectiveness Training (CET) program has demonstrated that coaches who engage in systematic self-monitoring and reflection create more positive sport environments, improve athlete well-being and self-esteem, and reduce dropout.

The CET approach emphasizes self-awareness (understanding your actual behaviors, not just your intentions), specific behavioral categories (tracking the concrete behaviors research shows matter most), continuous improvement, and athlete well-being as the reference point.

How to use this journal

  • Complete within two hours after each practice or game.
  • Be honest with yourself — this is for your growth, not external evaluation.
  • Focus on patterns over time, not perfection in any single session.
  • Set specific behavioral goals based on what you notice.
  • Consider sharing reflections with a mentor coach or peer for additional perspective.

Part 1 · Coaching behavior self-assessment

For each category, estimate the percentage of your coaching interactions that fit the description. Be as honest as possible.

A. Responses to desirable performance

1. Reinforcement
Positive, supportive reaction to good performance or effort. "Great job staying with that play!"		Percentage of positive moments I reinforced: %
2. Non-reinforcement
No response when an athlete does something well.		Percentage of positive moments with no response: %

B. Responses to mistakes

3. Mistake-contingent encouragement
"That's okay, shake it off." "No worries, next play."		Percentage of mistakes followed by encouragement: %
4. Mistake-contingent technical instruction
"Try stepping with your opposite foot next time."		Percentage of mistakes followed by technical instruction: %
5. Punishment
Negative reaction, criticism, yelling. "How many times do I have to tell you?"		Percentage of mistakes where I responded punitively: %
6. Punitive technical instruction
Instruction delivered in a hostile or harsh tone.		Percentage of instruction delivered punitively: %
7. Ignoring mistakes
No response at all.		Percentage of mistakes I did not respond to: %

Part 3 · Supporting basic psychological needs

Autonomy support

  • Did I give athletes meaningful choices today — selecting drills, setting goals, choosing roles?   ☐ Yes   ☐ No
  • Did I explain why we're doing things?   ☐ Provided rationales   ☐ Just told them what to do

Competence support

  • Were drills and challenges appropriately difficult?   ☐ Yes   ☐ Too easy for some   ☐ Too hard for some
  • Did I give specific, informational feedback about skill improvement?   ☐ Yes   ☐ Mostly vague praise   ☐ Mostly criticism

Relatedness support

  • Did I show genuine interest in athletes as people, not just performers?   ☐ Yes   ☐ No
  • Did I foster positive teammate interactions and team cohesion?   ☐ Yes   ☐ Somewhat   ☐ Didn't focus on this

Part 4 · Inclusive and equitable coaching

  • Did I give equal attention and quality instruction to all athletes regardless of ability?   ☐ Yes   ☐ More attention to high performers   ☐ More attention to struggling athletes
  • Did I notice or address exclusion, bullying, or discriminatory language today?   ☐ No issues observed   ☐ Observed and addressed   ☐ Observed but didn't address
